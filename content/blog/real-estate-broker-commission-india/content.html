There is no national, government-fixed real estate broker commission in India. In resale and rental deals the broker's fee is whatever the buyer, seller or tenant agrees to in writing. In new projects the developer pays the channel partner under its CP agreement. The RERA Act does not set a rate. One state rule does: in Haryana, property dealers may charge 1% of the agreed price, half from the seller and half from the buyer.

This guide explains how broker commission works in India, who pays it, how developer payouts to channel partners are usually structured, the RERA, GST and TDS rules that apply, and how to put it all in writing so nobody argues later.

How much do real estate brokers charge in India?

It depends on the kind of deal and, in a few places, on state rules:

  • Resale (buying or selling an existing home): usually a percentage of the sale price, agreed with the buyer, the seller or both. The rate is negotiable and varies by city, ticket size and how much work the broker does.
  • Rentals: usually linked to the rent, for example a share of one month's rent, agreed before the broker shows the property.
  • New projects (primary sales): the buyer normally pays the broker nothing. The developer pays the channel partner a commission set in its channel partner agreement.
  • Commercial leasing: usually linked to the rent and agreed with the owner, the tenant or both.

The one clear exception we know of is Haryana. Rule 10 of the Haryana Regulation of Property Dealers and Consultants Rules, 2009 allows a commission of 1% of the agreed consideration value, paid half by the seller and half by the buyer. In October 2020 the Haryana Real Estate Regulatory Authority said it would enforce this cap, according to an IANS report. If you deal in Gurugram or Faridabad, check the current position with HRERA before agreeing a fee.

For new projects, the commission is usually a percentage of the agreement value or the basic sale price, sometimes with higher slabs for more bookings or for selling during a launch window. Each developer writes its own terms, so compare offers line by line rather than by the headline percentage.

Who pays the broker: buyer, seller or developer?

New project: the developer. When you visit a site office with a channel partner, or register through a CP's link, the developer pays that CP. If a CP asks you for brokerage on a new-project booking, ask why in writing. It is not the usual arrangement.

Resale: whoever signed up with the broker. Often both sides pay, because the broker has worked for both: finding the property or the buyer, checking documents, negotiating and helping with registration. Agree the percentage, who pays it and when it is due before the first visit, and get it on paper or WhatsApp.

Rental: the tenant, the owner or both, as agreed in advance.

How developers pay channel partners

A channel partner in real estate works under a written agreement with the developer. The commission terms in those agreements usually cover:

  1. Base commission: a percentage of the agreement value or basic price for each booking the CP brings.
  2. Slabs or incentives: a higher rate after a set number of bookings, or for bookings made during a launch period.
  3. Payout milestones: when the commission becomes payable, for example after the buyer has paid a set share of the price or after the agreement for sale is registered.
  4. Lead registration rules: how a CP registers a buyer so the booking is credited to them, and what happens when two CPs claim the same buyer.
  5. Cancellation terms: what happens to commission already paid if the buyer cancels.

Payout timing matters as much as the rate. A CP agreement with a high percentage that pays only at possession can be worth less to a small broker than a lower rate paid at registration.

RERA rules on broker commission and agent registration

The Real Estate (Regulation and Development) Act, 2016 does not fix commission rates. It regulates who may act as an agent and how they must behave:

  • Section 9: registration. An agent must be registered with the state RERA authority before facilitating the sale or purchase of any plot, apartment or building in a project that has to be registered. Our guide to RERA registration for agents covers the process and published fees for UP, Haryana and Delhi.
  • Section 10: duties. A registered agent must not facilitate the sale of an unregistered project, must keep books of account and records, must not engage in unfair trade practices (such as false statements about the standard, quality or approvals of a property), and must help the buyer get the documents they are entitled to.
  • Section 62: penalty. An agent who breaks Section 9 or 10 can be fined up to ₹10,000 for every day of default, which can add up to 5% of the cost of the plot, apartment or building whose sale was facilitated.

Practical point: developers check a CP's RERA agent number before signing an agreement. An unregistered broker is not just at risk of a penalty, but is also in a weak position if a payout is disputed.

Taxes on brokerage: GST and TDS

GST. Brokerage is a taxable service and is charged at 18% GST. A broker must register for GST once their yearly turnover crosses the threshold (₹20 lakh for services in most states, ₹10 lakh in special category states), and then adds GST to the invoice. Check the current rate on the CBIC GST portal.

TDS. Tax is deducted at source on commission and brokerage. From 1 April 2026 the Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act, and the rule that used to be Section 194H now sits in Section 393(1) of the new Act. As published for FY 2026-27, the rate is 2% once the commission paid to one person crosses ₹20,000 in the year. It is higher (20%) if the broker hasn't given a PAN. The rate was 5% until 30 September 2024 and was cut to 2% from 1 October 2024. The ₹20,000 threshold applies from 1 April 2025; before that it was ₹15,000.

The broker gets the deducted amount as a tax credit, visible in their annual tax statement, and claims it when filing their return. Tax rules change often, so confirm the current section and rate with your chartered accountant or on the Income Tax Department portal.

What is a 70/30 commission split?

A commission split is how a brokerage firm and its individual agent divide a commission. In a 70/30 split, the agent keeps 70% of the commission on a deal and the firm keeps 30% to pay for the office, leads, software and support. The term comes mainly from the US market. In India, firm-agent arrangements vary widely: some pay salary plus incentive, others pay a share of each deal. Whatever the model, write it down.

Brokers vs your own ads: a developer's view

For a developer, channel partners bring something ads can't: people who already have buyers and are trusted by them. What CPs don't give you is a direct relationship with the buyer or data you own. Many developers do both: they work with CPs and also run their own Meta ads and Google ads to a project landing page, with fast WhatsApp follow-up.

To compare the two fairly, measure the cost per booking, not per lead: add up commission paid to CPs for a quarter, and separately add up ad spend plus agency and tools for the same quarter, then divide each by the bookings it produced. How to agree commission without disputes

Builders and developers:

  • Check the CP's RERA agent registration before signing, and quote it in the agreement and on invoices.
  • Write the rate, the payout milestone, the lead-registration rule and the cancellation rule in the agreement.
  • Pay against a proper invoice, deduct TDS and keep the records.

Home buyers:

  • On new projects, confirm in writing that the CP is paid by the developer.
  • On resale, agree the percentage, who pays and when it is due before the first visit.
  • Pay by bank transfer against a GST invoice if the broker is registered. Avoid cash.

Brokers: register with RERA, keep your agreements in writing and quote your RERA number on everything you send.
