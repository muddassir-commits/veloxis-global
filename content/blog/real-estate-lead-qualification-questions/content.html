Real estate lead qualification means finding out, in the first one or two conversations, whether a buyer can buy, wants to buy, and when. The quickest way is a short set of questions about purpose, budget, funding, configuration, location, timeline and who decides. Ask them in a natural order, write the answers down, and you will know which leads deserve a site visit today and which need a gentle follow-up for later.

This guide gives you the questions to ask, the exact wording that works on calls and WhatsApp in India, a simple scoring system, and the questions you should never ask.

Why qualifying leads matters

Ads and portals can bring a lot of enquiries, but not every enquiry is a buyer. Some people are curious, some are comparing prices for a relative, and some filled the form by mistake. If your sales team treats every lead the same way, the serious buyers wait while time goes to people who were never going to book.

Speed matters too. In a study published in Harvard Business Review (Oldroyd, McElheran and Elkington, 2011), firms that contacted online leads within an hour were nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead as firms that waited longer. Qualifying is not a reason to delay the first call: make the call quickly, and qualify during it.

Good qualification also makes your ads better. When you record why leads were not a fit (wrong budget, wrong location, just browsing), you can change the targeting, the form or the landing page so fewer of them arrive. Our real estate lead management guide shows how to track this.

The 10 core lead qualification questions

Many sales teams start from the BANT idea (budget, authority, need, timeline). For Indian real estate, these ten questions cover it in plain language:

  1. Purpose: "Are you buying for your own family to live in, or as an investment?"
  2. Configuration: "What size are you looking for: 2 BHK, 3 BHK, a plot?"
  3. Location: "Which areas are you considering? What matters most: office, school, metro, family nearby?"
  4. Budget: "What budget range are you comfortable with, all-inclusive?"
  5. Funding: "Will you take a home loan? Have you checked your eligibility with a bank yet?"
  6. Timeline: "When are you planning to buy: this month, in the next three months, or later this year?"
  7. Possession need: "Do you need a ready-to-move home, or is an under-construction project fine?"
  8. Decision makers: "Who else will be part of the decision? Will they come for the site visit too?"
  9. Current situation: "Are you renting right now, or do you need to sell a property first?"
  10. What they have seen: "Have you visited other projects? What did you like or not like?"

You don't need to ask all ten on the first call. Purpose, budget, timeline and decision makers tell you most of what you need. The rest can come naturally over WhatsApp or at the site visit.

Budget and home loan questions that get honest answers

"What is your budget?" often gets a vague answer. Two things help.

Give ranges. "Most of our 3 BHK units are between {{price A}} and {{price B}}. Is that in your range, or should I show you the 2 BHK options too?" A range is easier to answer than an open question, and it tells the buyer you are trying to fit them, not push them.

Ask about the down payment, not just the loan. Banks don't lend the full price. Under the Reserve Bank of India's loan-to-value limits for housing loans, a bank can lend up to 90% of the property value for loans up to ₹30 lakh, 80% for loans above ₹30 lakh and up to ₹75 lakh, and 75% above ₹75 lakh (Reserve Bank of India, Master Circular on Housing Finance). The buyer pays the rest from their own money, plus stamp duty and registration charges, which each state sets.

So a useful question is: "For the down payment and registration, roughly how much are you planning to put in yourself?" A buyer who has thought about this is usually serious. A buyer who hasn't may still be a good lead, just not for this month. Offering to connect them with a loan officer to check eligibility, with no obligation, is often the most helpful next step.

Timeline and decision-maker questions

Timeline separates "hot" from "later". Ask it in a way that is easy to answer honestly:

"Some buyers want to book this month, some are just starting to look. Where are you right now?"

Then find out who decides. In Indian families the decision often includes a spouse, parents or a sibling who is helping with money. If the decision maker isn't at the site visit, expect a second visit or a delay.

"Will {{your wife / your parents}} also be part of this decision? It's best if everyone can see the flat together, so I'll plan a time that works for all of you."

Finally, ask what could stop them: "Is there anything that would stop you from booking if you like the flat?" The answer (selling another property, waiting for a bonus, a job transfer) tells you exactly what to follow up on.

A simple way to score your leads

Use four labels, the same ones in your CRM, sheet or WhatsApp Business labels:

LabelWhat it meansNext step
HotBudget and configuration fit, buying within about three months, funding clear, decision makers knownBook a site visit within days; senior salesperson handles it
WarmGood fit but timeline is later, or loan or sale of another property still pendingRegular, useful follow-ups; invite to launch events or construction updates
ColdJust researching, budget far from your prices, or no reply after several triesOccasional updates only, if they agreed to receive them
Not a fitWrong location, wrong product, or asked not to be contactedClose the lead and record the reason

Record the reason for every "not a fit". After a few weeks, those reasons tell you what to change in your ads. If most poor leads have a budget far below your prices, for example, show the starting price range on the landing page and in the ad.

Qualify before the call: forms and WhatsApp

You can collect some answers before anyone picks up the phone.

  • Lead forms: on Meta lead ads, add one or two multiple-choice questions, such as configuration and buying timeline. Meta also offers a "higher intent" form type that adds a review step before submitting, which can reduce accidental submissions. Every extra question lowers the number of leads, so keep it short.   • Landing pages: a page that shows the price range, sizes and possession date answers the basic questions upfront, so the people who enquire already know the project fits. See our landing page guide.
  • WhatsApp: the first reply can ask one qualifying question, such as "for yourself or as an investment?". Our real estate WhatsApp message templates include first replies that do this.

Questions you should never ask

  • Religion, caste, community or diet. These have nothing to do with whether someone can buy a home. Asking them is wrong, offends good buyers and damages your brand.
  • Exact salary or bank balance on the first call. Ask about the budget range and the loan plan instead; the bank will check the details.
  • Pressure questions. "Can you pay the booking amount today?" on a first call pushes people away. Pressure belongs nowhere in the process.
  • Leading questions about returns. Never ask "Do you want guaranteed returns?" or promise appreciation. You can't guarantee it, and false claims can break RERA and consumer protection rules.

Putting it together: a sample first call

Here is how the questions flow in a two-to-three-minute call:

"Hi {{name}}, this is {{your name}} from {{company}}. You asked about {{project}} a few minutes ago. Is this a good time for two minutes?
Great. Are you looking for your own family or as an investment?
And which size: 2 BHK or 3 BHK?
Our 3 BHKs are between {{price A}} and {{price B}}, all-inclusive. Does that work for you?
When are you planning to buy?
Who else will be part of the decision?
Then let's plan a visit when everyone can come. Would Saturday or Sunday be better?"
