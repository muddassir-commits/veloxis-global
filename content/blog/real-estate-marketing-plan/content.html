A real estate marketing plan is a short written plan, usually for one project or one quarter, that says who you are selling to, what you will tell them, which channels you will use, how much you will spend, how enquiries will be handled and how you will measure results.

It fits on two or three pages. Its value is the decisions it forces before money is spent.

This guide gives you the 7 steps to write one, a one-page template you can copy, a worked example for a mid-size launch, a 90-day calendar, and a smaller version for individual agents and brokers. It is written for developers, channel partners and brokers in India, and it applies to apartments, plotted projects and commercial units alike.

Why does a real estate project need a marketing plan?

A project needs a marketing plan because launches are expensive and short: without one, budgets get spread across too many channels, messages change from ad to ad, and nobody owns the follow-up. A written plan gives the developer, the sales team, the agency and channel partners one shared answer to four questions: who is the buyer, what is the promise, where will we reach them, and how will we know it is working.

It also protects you legally. Every ad, brochure and hoarding has to match the registered project, and a plan is the place to check that once, before creative is made.

How to write a real estate marketing plan: 7 steps

  1. Set the goal in bookings, not leads. Start from the sales target, then work backwards with your own past numbers: site visits needed per booking and leads needed per visit.
  2. Define the buyer. Configuration, budget, where they live now, why they would move, and what worries them.
  3. Write the positioning. One sentence on why this buyer should choose this project, with proof.
  4. Choose the channels. Give each channel one job.
  5. Set the budget. A monthly amount and a test period per channel, with a reserve.
  6. Plan the follow-up. Who replies, how fast, with what, and how visits are booked.
  7. Decide what to measure and when to review. A weekly dashboard and a monthly budget review.

1. Set the goal in bookings

Write the target as bookings and a date, for example "sell the remaining units in Tower A by the end of the quarter". Then work back to the site visits and leads you need, using your own history: how many visits your team usually needs for one booking, and how many leads for one visit. If you have no history, write a first estimate, run the first month and correct it with real numbers. Avoid copying "industry averages" from blogs; they rarely match your city, price band or team.

2. Define the buyer

Describe the most likely buyer in plain words: configuration, budget, current home, reason to move, and main worries (possession date, loan approval, commute, builder trust). A project for first-time buyers in a growing suburb needs a different plan from one for NRI investors. If you sell to more than one group, plan each separately. Our guide to lead qualification questions helps you turn this into questions your team asks on the first call.

3. Write the positioning

One sentence on why this buyer should choose this project over the alternatives, with three proofs: location facts, the developer's delivery record, the price band or specification. This sentence drives every ad, brochure and sales conversation. See real estate branding and slogans and taglines.

5. Set the budget

Give each channel a monthly amount and a fixed test period, and keep part of the budget unassigned so you can move it to whatever works. Our real estate marketing budget guide explains how to calculate the total from your own cost per booking.

6. Plan the follow-up

Most plans stop at the ads. Write down who replies to enquiries, how fast, with what message, how leads are qualified and how site visits are booked. Speed matters: a study published in Harvard Business Review found that companies contacting online leads within an hour were far more likely to qualify them than those that waited longer. See lead management and how to book site visits.

7. Decide what to measure, and when to review

Pick a few numbers and review them weekly: leads, qualified leads, site visits and bookings by source, plus cost per visit and per booking. Hold a monthly review where budget moves from the weakest channel to the strongest, and name one owner for each number.

Example: a marketing plan for a mid-size launch

An illustration, not a real project or client. The numbers are placeholders to show the arithmetic; use your own history instead.

  • Project: a residential project of 2 and 3 BHK flats in a growing suburb, RERA-registered, possession date as declared on the RERA portal.
  • Goal: suppose the target is 30 bookings in the first four months. If your own records show roughly one booking for every 8 site visits, you need about 240 visits. If one in 6 leads usually visits, you need about 1,440 leads, or about 360 a month. If that looks unrealistic for the budget, change the goal or the plan now, not in month three.
  • Buyer: families renting within 10 km, buying their first or second home with a loan; main worries are possession date and EMI.
  • Positioning: "Homes 10 minutes from [metro station], from a developer with delivered projects in this city", with each claim matching the RERA filing and a real drive-time video as proof.
  • Channels: Meta lead ads and Google search for the project and locality names; two hoardings on the main commuter road; a small group of briefed channel partners; a project landing page with WhatsApp.
  • Follow-up: instant WhatsApp reply with the brochure and two questions; a call within the hour during office time; weekend visit slots offered as two choices.
  • Review: every Monday, leads, visits and bookings by source. After month one, the weakest campaign's budget moves to the strongest.

What should the first 90 days look like?

The first 90 days should move from preparation to testing to scaling, with a review at the end of each month.

What must a real estate marketing plan cover for RERA?

A real estate marketing plan must make sure every ad carries the project's RERA registration number and the authority's website, and that every claim matches the registered plans. Section 11 of the RERA Act, 2016 requires the registration number and authority website on advertisements, and Section 12 makes the promoter liable for false statements in them. Some states add rules: UP RERA has required a QR code on project ads (The Week, July 2024; check the current position on the UP RERA website). A marketing plan for real estate agents and brokers

An agent's plan is smaller but follows the same steps:

  • Pick a patch: one or two localities and one type of property you know better than anyone.
  • Be findable: a complete Google Business Profile, RERA agent number on everything, a simple website or listing page (local SEO guide).
  • Post weekly: listings, locality updates and short videos (Instagram Reels guide).
  • Choose one paid source to test, a portal package or a small Meta campaign, and track cost per deal.
  • Follow up every enquiry the same day and ask every happy client for a review and a referral.

Common marketing plan mistakes

  • A lead target with no booking target, so cheap, poor leads look like success.
  • Too many channels on a small budget.
  • No owner for follow-up, so leads wait until the next morning.
  • No source tracking, so nobody knows which channel produced bookings.
  • Creative that doesn't match the RERA filing, which is a legal risk as well as a trust problem.
  • Never reviewing the plan. A plan is a starting estimate; the monthly review is where it earns its keep.
