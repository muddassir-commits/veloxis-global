“Google or Meta?” is the question builders, brokers and channel partners ask us most. The honest answer is that they do different jobs, and most projects need both in different proportions. This guide compares Google Ads and Meta ads (Facebook and Instagram) for real estate in India: how each one finds buyers, where each one goes wrong, which to lead with for different projects, how to split a budget, and how to measure them fairly against each other.

The short answer

  • Google Search reaches people already searching for a project, locality or configuration. Fewer leads, usually closer to buying.
  • Meta (Facebook and Instagram) reaches people who aren’t searching yet. More leads, more filtering needed, and essential for launches and retargeting.

Put simply, Google captures demand that already exists, and Meta creates demand that didn’t exist yet. A project that only uses Google is limited by how many people happen to search. A project that only uses Meta pays to find buyers and then lets many of them go when they start searching and click a competitor’s search ad.

Google Ads: capturing buyers who are already searching

When someone types “3 BHK in Sector 150 Noida” or your project’s name, they have already decided what they want. Search ads let you appear at that moment with the right price and location.

Google works best when there is existing search demand: an established locality, a known project name, or ready-to-move inventory. For a brand-new project, project-name searches are near zero at first, so early Google volume comes from locality and configuration keywords. Negative keywords (rent, PG, jobs, resale if it’s a new launch) matter a lot, because property searches attract many irrelevant clicks.

The strongest Google campaigns for real estate usually have three parts:

  • Project name: protects your name from competitors and portals bidding on it. Cheap and high-converting once people know the project.
  • Locality and configuration: “2 BHK in [locality]”, “ready to move flats near [metro]”. This is where most new-project volume comes from.
  • Remarketing and YouTube: bringing back people who visited the landing page, with walkthrough videos.

Meta ads: reaching buyers before they search

Most people who could buy your flat aren’t searching today. Meta lets you put a walkthrough video or a price-band creative in front of them while they scroll. That makes it the main channel for launches, EOI drives and building a retargeting audience.

The trade-off is intent. Instant forms make enquiring very easy, so you also get curious scrollers. Qualifying questions, faster follow-up, and sending site-visit data back to Meta are what turn Meta leads into visits. If Meta’s Special Ad Category for housing applies to your ads, targeting options are limited, so the creative does more of the work.

Because targeting is broad, the creative is your targeting. A video that opens with the locality and the price band attracts people who care about that locality and can afford that price. A generic lifestyle video attracts everyone, and your sales team pays for it in wasted calls. Which to use when

  • New launch with little awareness: lead with Meta for volume and retargeting; run Google on locality and configuration searches.
  • Established project or known brand: Google on project-name and locality searches first; Meta for retargeting and offers.
  • Ready-to-move inventory: Google “ready to move” searches convert well; Meta retargeting to past enquiries.
  • Plotted developments: Meta video works well for showing the layout and surroundings; Google catches “plots in [locality]” searches.
  • Luxury projects: smaller, carefully targeted Meta audiences and YouTube for reach; Google for project-name and high-intent searches.
  • Channel partners with small budgets: Google on the specific projects you are mandated on, plus Meta retargeting of your page visitors.

How to split the budget

There is no correct split in advance; there is a sensible starting point and a rule for changing it. For a launch with little awareness, starting with more on Meta than Google makes sense, because there isn’t much search demand yet. Our launch plan uses roughly 60% Meta and 40% Google as a planning assumption. For a known project with steady searches, the balance often tips the other way.

The rule for changing it matters more than the starting point:

  1. Give each channel enough budget and time to show results, usually a few weeks, before judging it.
  2. Compare channels on cost per site visit, not cost per lead.
  3. Move budget weekly, in small steps, towards the channel and campaigns that produce visits more cheaply.
  4. Keep a small, fixed budget for retargeting on both, because it is usually the cheapest source of visits once traffic builds.

How much to spend in total depends on the project’s price, inventory and sales targets; our guide on how to generate real estate leads explains how to set targets by site visits rather than lead counts.

How the two work together

The best results usually come from using the channels as one system rather than rivals:

  • Meta creates searches. People who see a Meta video often search the project name on Google a few days later. Without a project-name search campaign, a competitor or portal may catch them.
  • Google visitors feed Meta retargeting. People who clicked a search ad but didn’t enquire can be shown walkthrough videos and offers on Instagram.
  • One landing page, one follow-up. Both channels should send buyers to the same fast project landing page, and every lead, from either channel, should get the same instant WhatsApp reply.

This is also why judging each channel on its own dashboard is misleading. A Meta campaign can look weak on its own numbers while it is quietly producing many of the project-name searches that Google gets credit for.

Measure both on site visits

Comparing channels on cost per lead is misleading: Meta leads look cheaper, but fewer of them visit. Compare Google and Meta on cost per site visit, using your CRM rather than the ad dashboards, and move budget weekly towards whichever produces visits more cheaply.

To do that, every lead needs a source tag from the ad it came from, and every site visit needs to be recorded against the lead. Send those visits back to both platforms, through Meta’s Conversions API and Google’s offline conversion import, so their algorithms learn what a buyer looks like instead of what a form-filler looks like.
