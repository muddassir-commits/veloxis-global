If your ads get clicks but few enquiries, the page is usually the problem. A real estate landing page has a few seconds, on a phone, to convince a buyer that the project is in the right place, at the right price, from a developer they can trust, and that asking for details is easy. These are the nine fixes we make most often on real estate landing pages for developers, brokers and channel partners, followed by a section order that works and a checklist to run before any campaign goes live.

1. Send ads to a dedicated project page

A corporate website has menus, other projects and an “About us” page, all distractions for someone who clicked on one project. Give each project, tower or phase its own page, and send each ad to the page that matches it.

“Matches” is more specific than it sounds. An ad about ready-to-move 3 BHKs should land on a page that leads with ready-to-move 3 BHKs, not the project’s general introduction. When the page repeats the promise the buyer just clicked on, they keep reading. When it doesn’t, they go back to the search results. For large projects, it is worth building separate versions of the page for the main configurations or buyer types, each with its own ads.

2. Answer the first five questions above the fold

On a phone, the first screen should say where the project is, what configurations are available, the starting price, whether it is RERA registered, and how to ask for more. Put the “Get price sheet on WhatsApp” button there too, and make sure the project brochure it sends matches the page.

A useful test: show the first screen to someone for five seconds, then take the phone away. If they can’t tell you the locality, the configurations and the starting price, the first screen isn’t doing its job. A beautiful render with the project name and a tagline fails this test more often than any other design.

3. Show a price, not “price on request”

Buyers use price to decide whether a project is worth their time. Hiding it brings in people who can’t afford the project and puts off those who can. A starting price or price band works; the full cost sheet can still be sent after they share their number.

Showing the price is also the simplest fix for junk leads. People outside your budget filter themselves out before they fill the form, so your sales team spends its time on buyers who can actually book. Label what the price includes, or say “all-inclusive” only if it truly is.

4. Make the RERA details easy to find

Show the project’s RERA registration number and the RERA website, and the QR code if your state requires it. For a buyer who doesn’t know the developer, it is the quickest proof that the project is legitimate. The law also requires it: advertisements must carry the registration number, and a landing page is an advertisement. Our guide to the RERA number check explains where else it must appear.

5. Sell the location

Buyers shortlist localities before projects. Show drive or walk times to the nearest metro, expressway, schools, hospitals and office hubs. A simple map with minutes beats a paragraph of adjectives.

Be honest with the numbers. “12 minutes to the metro” should be true at the time of day buyers travel, not at 3 a.m. Buyers check on Google Maps, and a claim that turns out to be wrong makes them doubt everything else on the page. Mention upcoming infrastructure only when it is officially approved, and say that it is upcoming.

6. Shorten the form and add WhatsApp

Ask only what your sales team needs: name, mobile number, configuration and budget. Add a sticky WhatsApp button on mobile; many buyers would rather chat than wait for a call. Then make sure every enquiry gets an instant reply; see how to automate lead follow-up.

Two small details make a noticeable difference. First, use buttons instead of typed fields for configuration and budget; tapping is faster than typing on a phone. Second, tell people what happens next, right on the button: “Get price sheet on WhatsApp” converts better than “Submit”, because it promises something specific and tells them where it will arrive.

7. Make it fast on a phone

Most property traffic from ads is mobile, often on patchy mobile data. Compress images, avoid sliders and auto-playing video above the fold, and load chat widgets and trackers after the page is usable. Test on a mid-range Android phone over 4G, and aim for Google’s “good” Core Web Vitals: the main content visible within 2.5 seconds, and no layout jumping as the page loads.

Speed also affects what you pay. Google Ads considers landing page experience when it decides how often your ads show and what you pay per click, and Meta shows fewer ads to people it expects to abandon slow pages. A slow page costs you twice: in lost visitors and in higher ad costs.

8. Show real progress, not just renders

Renders show what the project will be; dated construction photos show that it is happening. Add a construction-progress section, the developer’s past projects and bank approvals.

For buyers comparing several projects, this section often decides the shortlist. A short phone video of the site, dated and updated monthly, does more for trust than another render. If the developer has completed projects, show them with their locations, so buyers can visit or ask people who live there.

9. Track leads back to the ad

Set up conversion tracking for form submits, WhatsApp clicks and calls, and use a tagged link for each campaign and channel partner. Then feed site-visit outcomes from your CRM back to Google and Meta so they optimise for visits, not form fills.

Without this step you can only judge ads on cost per lead, which rewards the campaigns that produce the cheapest enquiries, not the ones that produce buyers. With it, you can see cost per site visit by campaign and move budget to what actually works.

A section order that works

Most high-converting project pages follow roughly the same order, because it follows the order of the buyer’s questions:

  1. Hero: project name, locality, configurations, starting price, RERA number and the main button.
  2. Why this project: three short reasons, each backed by a fact.
  3. Location: map with travel times.
  4. Floor plans: one card per unit type with carpet area, and a button to get the full plans.
  5. Amenities: the ones in the approved plan, with photos or renders labelled as such.
  6. Construction progress and developer track record.
  7. Payment plan: how much at booking, during construction and at possession.
  8. FAQs: the questions your sales team hears every day.
  9. Final call to action: the form again, WhatsApp, and site visit timings.

Repeat the call to action after every two or three sections, so a buyer who is ready never has to scroll back to the top.

Common landing page mistakes

  • Sending every ad to the homepage.
  • A slider of renders as the first screen, with no price or location.
  • Forms with eight or more fields, including email and “message”.
  • No WhatsApp option, so buyers who don’t want a call leave.
  • Different prices, possession dates or offers from the ads and brochure.
  • Pop-ups that cover the page before the buyer has read anything.
  • No tracking, so nobody knows which ad produced which buyer.

Checklist before the campaign goes live

  1. The first screen shows locality, configurations, starting price, RERA number and a clear button.
  2. The page matches the promise of the ads that point to it.
  3. The form asks for four things or fewer, with tap-to-choose options.
  4. A WhatsApp button is visible on mobile and every enquiry gets an instant reply.
  5. The page loads its main content within about 2.5 seconds on a mid-range phone.
  6. Travel times, amenities and dates are accurate and match the brochure.
  7. Conversion tracking works for forms, WhatsApp clicks and calls.
  8. Site-visit outcomes will be sent back to the ad platforms.
