Meta ads usually stop spending for one of five reasons: a payment or spending-limit block on the ad account, an ad that is rejected, in review or switched off, a schedule or spend limit that has run out, a budget too small for the optimisation event, or a bid cap, narrow audience or overlapping ad sets that keep your ads out of auctions.

This guide is for Indian D2C and e-commerce founders, in-house marketers and small agencies whose Facebook or Instagram campaign shows "Active" but spends nothing, or spends far less than its budget. Most guides that rank for "Meta ads not spending" list a dozen causes in random order. This one puts them in the order you should check them, from the account level down to the single ad, and links each rule to Meta's own Business Help Center page so you can confirm it yourself.

Ads Manager labels and Meta's rules change often. Everything below was checked against Meta's Business Help Center on 16 November 2026.

Is your Meta campaign really not spending, or just pacing?

First rule out normal behaviour: a new or edited ad can take up to a day to start, and a daily budget is a weekly average, so some days spend less than others. Not every quiet day is a problem.

Three things often look like "not spending" when the system is working as designed:

  • The ad is still being reviewed or prepared. Meta says most ads are reviewed within 24 hours, sometimes longer. Its troubleshooting pages also say you "may need to wait up to 24 hours to see delivery", and that an ad which has passed review but not yet started shows the status Preparing.
  • Daily budgets flex across the week. According to Meta's page on daily budgets, your daily budget is an approximate amount over a week running Sunday to Saturday. Meta may spend up to 75% over it on days with better opportunities, but never more than 175% of it in one day or 7 times it in one week. The flip side is that some days spend less than the daily budget.
  • Changes made late in the day. Meta's guide for campaigns that slowed down or stopped gives the example of doubling a daily budget at 10pm: the system only has two hours to spend the increase, so that day may under-spend and the next day catch up.

So before you change anything, look at spend over the last seven days by day, not just today. If the week as a whole is close to seven times your daily budget, there is nothing to fix. If spend is zero, or the whole week is far below budget, work through the checks below in order.

In what order should you check a Meta campaign that isn't spending?

Check from the top down: the ad account first, then the campaign, then each ad set, then each ad. A block at a higher level stops everything below it, and Meta notes that a campaign can look active while its ad set or ad is stuck, so you need to look at every level.

Meta's own Troubleshoot ad delivery guide splits the problem the same way: start at the campaign level if you use Advantage+ campaign budget, at the ad set level if you set ad set budgets, and at the ad level if the problem is still there. Use this order:

  1. Account and billing: failed payment, ad account spending limit, Meta's daily spending limit, a disabled or restricted account.
  2. Delivery column, at all three levels: Rejected, In review, Processing, Preparing, Off, No ads, Scheduled, Completed.
  3. Schedule and spend limits: start and end dates, ad scheduling hours, lifetime budget, campaign and ad set spending limits.
  4. Budget versus optimisation event: is the daily budget big enough for the result you asked Meta to find?
  5. Bid strategy: bid cap, cost per result goal or ROAS goal set too tight.
  6. Audience and structure: audience too narrow, ad sets overlapping, too many ads splitting the budget.
  7. If it spent before and then slowed: recent edits in activity history, creative fatigue, audience saturation.

Is a payment or spending limit pausing your whole ad account?

If every campaign in the account stopped at the same moment, the cause is almost always at account level: a failed payment, an ad account spending limit you set, or the daily spending limit Meta sets. These are the first things to rule out because nothing else matters until they are clear.

Failed payment

Meta's page on fixing a failed payment says that when your payment method fails, your ads are paused and your ad account is disabled until you pay the amount due. Several of the causes it lists are very common for Indian cards and accounts:

  • the card is not enabled for online transactions;
  • the card or payment method is not connected to a phone number you can receive the OTP on;
  • the card has expired, has a per-transaction limit or lacks funds near your billing date;
  • the card type is not supported: Meta notes that American Express cards are not supported in India;
  • the same payment method is added to too many ad accounts (Meta says one method can typically be added to up to 10).

Fix it in Billing & payments by paying the balance or adding funds, and add a backup payment method so one declined charge doesn't stop your sale-week campaigns. Meta also notes that advertisers in India may not be able to use Pay now or Add funds to clear the balance once the account is disabled, which is one more reason to keep a working backup method on file before anything fails. If a card has a per-transaction limit, Meta suggests lowering your payment threshold to match it.

Ad account spending limit

This one catches many teams because Ads Manager does not make it obvious. Meta's page on ad account spending limits says that when you reach the limit, your ads pause and you get a notification, "but ads will still be listed as active in Meta Ads Manager." If a freelancer or a past agency set a limit months ago, your campaigns can look perfectly healthy while spending nothing. Change, reset or remove the limit in Payment settings.

Daily spending limit set by Meta

Separately, Meta sets a daily spending limit for most advertisers based on advertising and payment history. When you reach it, ads pause and resume the next day after the limit resets at midnight in your ad account's time zone. Meta says the limit usually rises on its own as you build history, follow its advertising policies and spend up to or near the limit, and it may be lowered for a new business portfolio, ad account or Page, or after policy problems. A brand-new account for a new D2C label is the classic case: the campaigns are fine, but the account is not yet allowed to spend what the budgets ask for.

Your ads run each day until they hit the lowest of three numbers: Meta's daily spending limit, your ad account spending limit and your daily budget. Check all three in Payment settings.

What does the Delivery column say for the campaign, ad set and ad?

Open the Delivery column at all three levels, because the campaign can show Active while the ad set or ad below it is rejected, in review or off. Meta's page on delivery statuses recommends checking every level for exactly this reason.

Two details matter for e-commerce teams. First, editing an ad's targeting, creative, optimisation or billing event starts a new review, while changes to bid amount, budget or schedule do not. So swapping the creative on a running ad the night before a sale can send it back into review at the worst time. Second, ads can be reviewed again after they go live, so an ad that ran for a week can still be rejected later. Check Account Quality for rejected ads and account restrictions, and follow Meta's page on troubleshooting a rejected ad if you think a rejection is wrong. Product categories with health, weight or before-and-after claims are worth extra care when you write the copy.

Are schedules, end dates or spend limits blocking delivery?

Yes, often: an ad set that only runs a few hours a day, a campaign past its end date, a used-up lifetime budget or a restrictive spend limit can all stop spend while everything else looks fine. Open the Budget & schedule section of the campaign and each ad set.

Meta's guide for a campaign that isn't running lists these checks:

  • Campaign spending limit and lifetime budget: if either is already used up, the campaign stops until you raise it.
  • Budget scheduling: make sure the campaign and ad sets are scheduled to run now, and look for gaps between schedules.
  • Restrictive hours: if you only allow ads in a small window (say, evenings), Meta suggests relaxing the schedule so there is more time to deliver.
  • Ad set spend limits: minimum or maximum spend limits on ad sets inside an Advantage+ campaign budget can block delivery. Meta suggests removing all spend limits as a test, noting that changes usually take up to 15 minutes to apply and delivery may take a few hours to improve.

If you recently extended a campaign's end date, Meta also notes that the system re-plans pacing over the new, longer period, so daily spend can drop because the same lifetime budget is now spread over more days.

Is your budget too small for the result you optimise for?

A common cause of under-spend is a daily budget that is small compared with the cost of the result you asked Meta to optimise for; Meta's own rule of thumb is a daily budget of at least 10 times the average cost of that optimisation event. Purchases cost more than add-to-carts, which cost more than landing page views.

Meta's guides for an ad set that isn't running and an ad set that hasn't delivered enough both give that 10x guideline, with their own example: if your average cost per link click is $5, your daily budget should be at least $50. Meta's best practices for minimum budgets add that if you use a cost per result goal, your daily budget should be at least 5 times that goal.

For a D2C store this usually shows up in one of two ways:

  • A small budget optimised for Purchase. If the ad set gets very few purchases a week, the system has little to learn from, spends cautiously and may show Learning limited. Meta's page About learning limited says an ad set becomes learning limited when it is unlikely to get about 50 optimisation events in the week after its last significant edit.
  • The budget split across many small ad sets. One ad set with the whole budget gathers results faster than five ad sets with a fifth each.

You have three honest options: raise the budget so it fits the event, combine ad sets so one ad set gets the whole budget, or, as Meta suggests, optimise for an event that happens more often, such as add to cart instead of purchase, while you build volume. Before choosing, add the Optimisation events and cost per result columns in Ads Manager so you are working from your own numbers rather than a guess. Broken purchase tracking makes this worse, because Meta can't learn from purchases it never sees: test that every order reaches Meta once, as covered in our D2C brand launch checklist.

Are a bid cap, cost per result goal or ROAS goal holding spend back?

If you use a bid cap, cost per result goal or ROAS goal, a setting that is too strict is one of the most likely reasons spend stays far below budget, because Meta will skip auctions it can't win within your limit. If you use highest volume bidding with no cap or goal, skip this check.

Meta's troubleshooting pages explain the difference: a bid cap is the most you are willing to bid in an auction, while a cost per result goal is the average you want to pay for your optimisation event and "should align with your average cost per result in Ads Manager reporting". For ROAS goals, Meta says to set the goal low enough to be reachable. When other advertisers bid harder, for example in the weeks around Diwali or big marketplace sales, a cap that worked last month may no longer be competitive. Meta's slowed-down campaign guide says the fix may be to raise or remove the cap, or choose an optimisation event higher up the funnel.

A clean way to test it: duplicate the ad set, remove the cap or goal on the copy, and compare spend and cost per result over the next few days. Meta notes that changes usually take up to 15 minutes to apply and that delivery may take a few hours to improve if the cap was the constraint. If you hover over a Learning limited status and see a tooltip about low bid or cost control, Meta is telling you directly that this is the reason.

Is the audience too narrow, or are your own ad sets competing?

A very narrow audience gives Meta too few people to show the ad to, and several ad sets aimed at the same people block each other: Meta only lets one of your ads into each auction. Both lead to ad sets that spend a little each and never reach budget.

On overlap, Meta's page Understand auction overlap explains that when two or more of your ads enter the same auction, Meta chooses the one with the highest total value and the others are not considered. That stops you bidding against yourself, but it also means overlap "can prevent an ad set from spending its full budget or achieving enough results to exit the learning phase". Meta adds that running ads from separate ad accounts may not avoid it, since it can recognise them as the same advertiser. Its suggested fixes are to consolidate accounts, combine similar ad sets with their budgets, or turn off the overlapping ad sets that are learning limited or have the fewest results and move their budget to the one that stays on.

On volume, Meta's page About managing ad volume says that when an advertiser runs too many ads at once, each ad delivers less often, fewer exit the learning phase and more budget is spent before the system can optimise. It recommends combining ad sets and using breakdowns for reporting instead of separate ad sets per audience, region, placement or language, and putting several creative assets into one ad rather than making many ads. For catalogues with many products, Meta points to Advantage+ catalog ads.

For a typical Indian D2C account, that often means replacing a stack of interest-based ad sets (one per interest, one per city, one per age band) with fewer, broader ad sets that each have enough budget to learn. If you test creators' videos, keep them as variations inside fewer ads, as in our guide to UGC ads for D2C brands.

Your campaign was spending, then slowed down or stopped: what changed?

When a campaign that used to spend slows down, the answer is usually in its recent history: an edit just before the drop, a creative people have seen too often, or an audience that has already seen your ads without acting. Meta's slowed-down guide covers each of these.

  1. Find the day it changed. Use the Day breakdown in Ads Manager to see exactly when spend or results dropped.
  2. Read the activity history. Look for edits made just before that day. Meta names frequent pauses during the day ("extensive pausing can interfere with the system's ability to optimize delivery"), budget or schedule changes near the end of the day, and schedule extensions.
  3. Check for creative fatigue. When Meta believes your audience has seen the same ad too many times, the Delivery column shows Creative limited or Creative fatigue for the ad set or ad. Fresh creative is the fix.
  4. Check for audience saturation. Meta describes this as a high share of your audience seeing your ads without taking action, and suggests a larger audience.
  5. Check the bid cap again. If cost per result is rising because competition went up, a cap that used to work can now block spend.

Doing this by hand across dozens of ads is slow. If nothing on this page fixes it, Meta's guidance is that once an ad has been published for at least 48 hours and none of the checks work, you can delete and recreate the ad; if more than one ad in the campaign is not delivering, contact Meta's support team.

How do you stop this from happening again?

Most spend problems are preventable with a short weekly routine: a working backup payment method, no forgotten spending limits, fewer and broader ad sets with enough budget for their event, caps that match current costs, and creative refreshed before fatigue shows.

  • Check Payment settings once a week: spending limit, daily spending limit, payment method expiry.
  • Keep the Delivery column visible at all three levels and look at it before and after every edit.
  • Batch edits instead of changing things several times a day, and avoid big budget changes late at night.
  • Review spend by day for the past week, not just today, so normal pacing doesn't trigger panic edits.
  • Keep a supply of new creative ready, so a fatigued ad can be replaced the same day.
